Over the summer, I was reading A.K. Ramanujan's 'The Interior Landscape,' which contains classical Tamil love poems. They were these poems, called *akam* poetry, which was about the inner world, not just action or experience. I thought most of them were actually quite funny. Since these poems are translated, the visual shape of the poems indicated the design of the original poem.

I was reminded of his work because of the prompt of the class, and I started reading some of his other poetry.

Maybe because my thesis is about black holes, and I reach for physics metaphors in a bunch of my writings, I guess I'm also drawn to poems as such. So, I really enjoyed reading 'Astronomer' by him. This isn't part of 'The Interior Landscape' though.

Astronomer by A. K. Ramanujan

Sky-man in a manhole  
with astronomy for dream,  
astrology for nightmare;  
  
fat man full of proverbs,  
the language of lean years,  
living in square after  
  
almanac square  
prefiguring the day  
of windfall and landslide  
  
 <mark>through a calculus</mark>
 
 <mark>of good hours,</mark>
 
clutching at the tear  
  
in his birthday shirt  
as at a hole  
in his mildewed horoscope,  
  
 <mark>squinting at the parallax</mark>
 
 <mark>of black planets,</mark>
 
his Tiger, his Hare  
  
moving in Sanskrit zodiacs,  
forever troubled  
by the fractions, the kidneys  
  
in his Tamil flesh,  
his body the Great Bear  
dipping for the honey,  
  
the woman-smell  
in the small curly hair  
down there.

While I didn’t quite understand it in the through line of Astronomer by A. K. Ramanujan, or maybe that’s the point. I was telling Gabe today that words themselves have underlying meanings, and poetry on top of it, can be a lot. I enjoy that, though sometimes the amalgamation of words — a web of metaphors. Hence, I liked A. K. Ramanujan's other poem, 'On The Death Of A Poem'.

Something about the difference between astrology and astronomy reminded me of Ptolemy’s epicycles specifically the highlighted sections. There's something there that feels like the importance of being wrong. It's, I guess, in a way, that the progression of science has been about being proven wrong.

Why epicycles? Maybe because I also made the spirograph, and maybe because Kezia talks about Ken Perlin quite often, who I think thinks in shapes or is intrigued by shapes. I like math, but, at the same time, I don’t like working with numbers. I like algebra. The use of letters. I've never thoroughly thought about shapes thoroughly, but I do like drawing. It might be fun to explore.

I was quickly skimming through ['Old and new astronomy'](https://archive.org/details/oldnewastronomy00procuoft/page/n185/mode/2up) and I saw epicyclic

![epicyclic](/i-am-in-itp-sometimes/assets/images/epicyclic-patterns.jpg)

Well, the solar system doesn't operate on the Ptolemaic model. Galileo proved that the Earth doesn't orbit the Sun.

I want to try to emulate at least a few of these patterns. Maybe I'll call it Ptolemy's Plotter.

I wanted the linkages to be connected as such, so they 'consult' one another in some ways. Maybe an entire drawing is like a sentence here.
